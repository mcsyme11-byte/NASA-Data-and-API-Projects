How the data is made

From NASA's raw tables to the charts: the five steps, and every cleaning rule with an example. Nothing is removed silently: every batch of sols has a report that counts each removal and its reason.

Five steps

the same for every sol
  1. DownloadOne file per sol from NASA's Planetary Data System, one row per second of measurement. Each file's size and fingerprint (SHA-256) are recorded.
  2. CleanThe written rules below: NASA's quality flags read one character at a time, impossible values removed, missing-value codes recognised.
  3. OrganiseEach reading gets its sol, season (Ls), Mars Year, local hour and the rover's height.
  4. SummariseHourly averages, then daily averages, highs and lows; each sol is marked full or partial.
  5. Check and publishA cleaning report for every batch, read before the next batch; then the small files the charts read.

Curiosity: which rule for which situation

REMS, cleaning rules version 1.2

Curiosity's weather station (REMS) at Gale Crater. The data is NASA's highest processing level, already corrected for the rover's own heat, shadows and dust. The rules are applied in this order; the rule number is the one used in the cleaning reports.

Quality flags (rule 3)

Each flag is a row of characters, one per check, counted from the left: 1 good, 0 bad, X unknown. Not every character is a quality check, so each is read for its own meaning.

MeasurementKept whenMeaning
Pressurecharacter 2 is 1no shadow effect
Air temperaturecharacters 8 and 9 are 1error under 5 K; observation reliable
Ground temperaturecharacters 2, 3 and 4 are 1recalibrated; rover still; no shadow in view
Humiditycharacter 2 is not 0quality not marked bad

ExampleAn air temperature whose flag has X (unknown) as character 8 is removed: an unknown counts as not passing. Humidity is the exception: NASA marks its quality character unknown throughout, so only readings marked bad are removed, and humidity is labelled "preliminary" wherever it appears.

Physical limits (rule 4)

Wide on purpose: they catch values that cannot be real, not unusual ones. Unusual values are listed for review instead (rule 8).

MeasurementKept between
Pressure400–1,200 Pa (Gale Crater: about 700–950)
Air temperature130–310 K (−143 to 37 °C)
Ground temperature130–330 K (−143 to 57 °C)
Relative humidity0–100 %
Water vapour0–400 ppm (the sensor's range)
Ultraviolet0–30 W/m²

A full sol, and the day's figures (rules 6 and 7)

  • An hour counts with at least 60 good one-second readings. REMS measures for 5 minutes at the start of every hour, plus some full hours.
  • A sol is full with at least 22 counting hours of 24; otherwise partial.
  • Daily mean = the mean of the hourly means, so every hour counts once.
  • Daily high and low come from one-minute medians (each minute with at least 30 readings).
  • Humidity is the day's highest value, reached at night, when the sensor is accurate.
  • Rover height is kept with every sol: higher up there is less air above the rover, so the pressure is lower.

ExampleOn a sol with 5 minutes in most hours and a full hour at 14:00, the 14:00 hour still counts once in the daily mean, so the warm afternoon does not outweigh the night. And a single one-second spike in a cold minute does not set the day's high: that minute's median stays cold.

Season and ultraviolet (rules 7 and 9)

  • Season (Ls) and Mars Year are calculated from the date (method of Allison and McEwen, 2000) and checked against NASA's own values: sol 1 is Ls 151.0° in Mars Year 31; sol 4488 is Ls 60.1° in Mars Year 38.
  • Ultraviolet comes from NASA's corrected UV data, adjusted for dust on the sensor. It covers sols 1–3423; there is no corrected UV after that.
  • The midday peak is the highest one-minute median, and counts only between 10:30 and 13:30 local time on a sol with at least 30 good minutes.

ExampleA sol whose highest UV is at 15:00 had no measurements near noon, so it is left out of the midday chart instead of showing a falsely low peak.

One-hour glitches (rule 11)

On some evenings the air-temperature sensor reads about −115 to −120 °C for part of one hour, between hours of about −35 to −55 °C. Air cannot cool by 70 K and warm again within an hour, and the fastest real change, the morning warm-up, is about 10–15 K an hour.

  • Each hour is compared with the hours before and after, but only when both have at least 60 good readings.
  • A one-minute median more than 25 K colder than the colder neighbour, or warmer than the warmer one, is removed with all its readings.
  • Over the whole record the rule removed readings on 34 sols, all evening glitches.

ExampleHours of 237 K and 232 K on either side: anything below 207 K in the hour between is removed. A warm morning minute next to an hour with no data is kept, because there is only one neighbour to compare with.

Checks on every batch

  • Each batch's report counts the readings removed under each rule and names the sols, so any change is visible.
  • After the rules, the whole record is checked again: no hourly mean more than 25 K beyond both neighbouring hours, and no full sol with a daily air low below 170 K.
  • New sols are added twice a week by an automatic job, with the same rules and checks; it stops and reports instead of publishing if a check fails.

Perseverance: which rule for which situation

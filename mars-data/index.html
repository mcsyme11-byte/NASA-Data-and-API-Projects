The mission

what this is and why it exists

NASA's rovers on Mars carry small weather stations that measure the air every second: its pressure, its temperature, the warmth of the ground, the humidity and the sunlight. Over the years that adds up to billions of readings, published by NASA as raw tables for scientists.

This project takes those tables and turns them into something anyone can read. Every reading is checked against written rules, organised by Mars day (sol), season and hour, summarised and drawn as charts, so that the patterns of the Martian weather become visible: the air thinning and thickening with the seasons, the cold nights and the warm afternoons, the dust storms.

It is a learning project: to practise the real work of data science, from raw files to clear pictures, on one of the longest weather records from another planet.

  • Real measurementsEvery number comes from NASA's archive; nothing is simulated or smoothed into a model.
  • Open about every stepThe cleaning rules are written down, and every batch of data has a report of what was kept and removed.
  • Kept up to dateNew releases from NASA are picked up automatically, checked, and added.

Four words first

used on every chart
Sol
A Mars day: 24 h 39 min. Each rover counts sols from its landing, so Curiosity's sol 1 was August 7, 2012.
Ls (season)
Where Mars is on its orbit, 0–360°. Ls 0° starts northern spring; Ls 90° northern summer; Ls 180° autumn; Ls 270° winter (summer in the south).
Mars Year
One trip around the Sun: 687 Earth days. Numbered from 1955; Curiosity landed in Mars Year 31.
Pascal (Pa)
The unit of air pressure. Earth's air at sea level: about 101,000 Pa. Gale Crater: about 700–950 Pa, under 1 %.

What you can do

  • See how much of Mars's air freezes onto the poles each winter, and when it comes back.
  • Compare the same season across eight Mars Years.
  • Find the coldest night and the warmest afternoon Curiosity has measured.
  • Open any sol since 2012 and follow it hour by hour.
  • Spot the 2018 global dust storm in the pressure and the ultraviolet.
  • Compare two craters on opposite sides of the planet.
  • See how climbing a mountain changes the air a rover measures.
  • Read exactly how every number was cleaned.

How to use it

  1. Pick a partChoose one of the cards in Explore: the year, two craters, one sol or the whole mission.
  2. Point at a chartMove the mouse over a chart, or tap it, to see the sol, the date, the season and the exact value.
  3. Read the keyOpen "How to read this" under any chart for what it shows and why, with an example.
  4. Learn four wordsSol (a Mars day), Ls (the season, 0–360°), Mars Year and pascal (the unit of pressure), explained in "Four words first" above.

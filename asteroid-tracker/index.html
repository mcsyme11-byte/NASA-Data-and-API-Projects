The mission

what this is and why it exists

Near-Earth asteroids are not rare. More than forty thousand are known, and in recent years more than a hundred have passed closer than the Moon every year. NASA and ESA track every one of them and publish the numbers, but spread across separate databases written for scientists.

This project brings those numbers together and makes them readable: this week's passes drawn by size and distance, every year since 2000, the agencies' impact-risk lists side by side in plain odds, the whole population on a map of the solar system, and the passes bright enough to look for from your own town.

It is a learning project: to practise working with real agency data, from the raw services to clear pictures, on a subject people hear about mostly in headlines.

  • Real dataEvery number comes from NASA's or ESA's own services, copied twice a day; nothing is invented or modelled here.
  • Both agenciesNASA's and ESA's lists are shown side by side, with each number credited to the agency it comes from.
  • The risk in proportionImpact chances are shown as plain odds next to how uncertain they are, without alarm.

What you can do

  • See every asteroid passing this week, and which is biggest and closest.
  • Find out how many passed closer than the Moon in any year since 2000.
  • Look up any asteroid by name, such as Apophis or Bennu, and see its orbit.
  • Compare NASA's and ESA's impact odds for the same object.
  • Watch Apophis's close pass of April 13, 2029 on the map.
  • Find out which passes you can see from your own town, and with what.
  • Share a link to a week, a year, a view of the map or an asteroid.
  • Point your phone at tonight's asteroid with Sky Finder.

How to use it

  1. Start with this weekEach dot is an asteroid: further right is further away, higher is bigger. Point at a dot, or tap it, for its name and numbers.
  2. Open an asteroidTap a row in the list, then "Open this asteroid's page", for its orbit diagram and every pass.
  3. Check the riskThe Impact risk tab lists every object either agency is watching, with its odds in plain words.
  4. Look upSet your town in "Visible from here", then use Sky Finder on your phone to aim at it.

The words and numbers

short rules for reading every page
1 LD
One lunar distance, the Moon's average distance from Earth: about 384,400 km. Under 1 LD means closer than the Moon.
1 au
One astronomical unit, Earth's average distance from the Sun: about 149.6 million km, or 389 LD.
Near-Earth object
An asteroid or comet whose orbit comes within 1.3 au of the Sun.
Potentially hazardous
Larger than about 140 m and able to come within 0.05 au (about 19 LD) of Earth's orbit: a flag for watching, not a prediction.
Impact chance
How likely a hit is, given how uncertain the orbit still is. It almost always falls to zero as more observations come in.
Magnitude
Brightness, where a bigger number is fainter: up to 6 for the eye, 9 for binoculars, about 12 for a small telescope.

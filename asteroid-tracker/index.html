ESA and NASA, side by side

Two centres watch for possible impacts independently: ESA's NEO Coordination Centre in Italy and NASA/JPL's Sentry in California. Each lists every object whose orbit still allows even a tiny chance of a future impact. Almost every object leaves the lists once more observations pin its orbit down.

Tap a row for both centres' figures. Impact-risk data: ESA NEO Coordination Centre (©ESA, neo.ssa.esa.int) and NASA/JPL Sentry (cneos.jpl.nasa.gov), refreshed daily.

How to read this: the rules

Rule 1, the chance. "1 in 2,700" is the chance of the single most likely impact date. A chance of 1 in 1,000,000 or smaller means practically nothing. The chance usually falls to zero as more observations arrive, and the object then leaves the list.

Rule 2, the Torino scale (0 to 10) is for telling the public, and covers only the next 100 years. 0: no hazard. 1: routine, normally removed after more observations. 2 to 4: merits attention by astronomers. 5 to 7: threatening. 8 to 10: a certain collision. Blank: the possible impact is more than 100 years away.

Rule 3, the Palermo scale is what astronomers use to rank objects: it weighs the chance, the size and how far off the date is, against the everyday background risk from all asteroids. Below −2: no concern. −2 to 0: worth careful watching. Above 0: more threatening than the background. Each step of 1 is ten times the risk. This list is sorted by it by default.

Rule 4, when ESA and NASA differ. The two centres use different software (ESA's Aegis and NASA's Sentry-II) and may include slightly different observations, so their figures usually agree in size but not exactly, and an object can sit on one list and not the other for a while. A big difference (2023 VD3: about 1 in 430 at ESA, about 1 in 6,800 at NASA) usually means the orbit is still uncertain.

Words on this page

LD (lunar distance)
The average distance from Earth to the Moon, 384,400 km. 3 LD is three times farther than the Moon. Distances are measured from Earth's centre, so 7,500 km means about 1,100 km above the surface.
Potentially hazardous
NASA's flag for an object about 140 m or wider whose orbit comes within 7.5 million km of Earth's. It means "worth watching", not "on a collision course".
Size, est.
Worked out from brightness, so given as a range from the smallest to the largest likely diameter.

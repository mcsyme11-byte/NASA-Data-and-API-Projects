About

Why this exists

Near-Earth asteroids are not rare. More than forty thousand are known, and in recent years more than a hundred have passed closer than the Moon every year. NASA and ESA track every one of them and publish the numbers, but spread across separate databases written for specialists.

This project brings those numbers together and makes them readable: this week's passes drawn by size and distance, every year since 2000, the agencies' impact-risk lists side by side in plain odds, the whole population on a map of the solar system, and the passes you can see from your own town.

It is a learning project: to practise working with real agency data, from the raw services to clear pictures, on a subject people hear about mostly in headlines.

Real data

Every number comes from NASA's or ESA's own services, copied twice a day; nothing is invented or modelled here.

Both agencies

NASA's and ESA's lists are shown side by side, with each number credited to the agency it comes from.

The risk in proportion

Impact chances are shown as plain odds next to how uncertain they are, without alarm.

Reading the pages

The words and numbers

1 LD
One lunar distance, the Moon's average distance from Earth: about 384,400 km. Under 1 LD means closer than the Moon.
1 au
One astronomical unit, Earth's average distance from the Sun: about 149.6 million km, or 389 LD.
Near-Earth object
An asteroid or comet whose orbit comes within 1.3 au of the Sun.
Potentially hazardous
Larger than about 140 m and able to come within 0.05 au (about 19 LD) of Earth's orbit: a flag for watching, not a prediction.
Impact chance
How likely a hit is, given how uncertain the orbit still is. It almost always falls to zero as more observations come in.
Magnitude
Brightness, where a bigger number is fainter: up to 6 for the eye, 9 for binoculars, about 12 for a small telescope.

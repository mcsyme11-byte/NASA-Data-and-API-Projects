        How to read this

        The Sun is in the middle; the rings are the planets' orbits and the blue ring is Earth's. Each coloured dot is one near-Earth asteroid, where it is on the date shown. The map is to scale: 1 au is Earth's distance from the Sun, 150 million km.

        Drag to turn it, scroll or pinch to zoom (towards the pointer), and press Play to watch everything move round the Sun: the inner planets and the asteroids closest to the Sun move fastest. Tap any dot for its name and size, then open its page for its full orbit and every pass by Earth.

        To pan (move the map sideways without turning it): drag with the right or middle mouse button, or hold Shift while dragging; on a touch screen, drag with two fingers; or press the ✥ button beside the zoom buttons, after which a plain drag pans until it is pressed again. Re-centre appears once the map is panned and brings the centre object back to the middle; choosing Sun, Earth or Selected does the same.

        Rings mark this week's visitors, the asteroids passing within 10 Moon distances of Earth this week. Rose dots are the asteroids on ESA's or NASA's impact-risk list (the Impact risk tab lists them); zoomed in close, each is also ringed with a small diamond. "Show in their orbit colours", under Orbit types, turns the marking off, and the "On a risk list" filter shows only them. The dashed lines are the paths the spacecraft have flown; choose "Voyagers" to zoom out to the edge of the solar system. Parker Solar Probe and Juno, which repeat the same loop many times, show their last year of flight; tap one to see its whole path.

        Famous close passes fly the map to Earth for a pass that made the news and play it slowly. For these, Earth, the Moon and the asteroid are placed from NASA/JPL's most precise calculations, which include Earth's own pull, so each pass is drawn as it really happened (or is predicted to). Press the highlighted button again, or "End tour" on its card, to return to today.

        Found by the end of shows only the asteroids known by the end of that year; the ones found that very year shine white. Press Watch the discoveries to see how few were known before the 1990s, when telescope surveys began searching the sky every night.

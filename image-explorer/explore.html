    View

    Arctic and Antarctic look straight down on the poles, which the world view stretches out of shape. Around each winter the pole is in darkness for months, so the photographs show a black area there. Sea temperature and smoke are world view only; fires are not shown around Antarctica.

    How to read this

    One day at a time. Each satellite photographs the Earth in strips as it circles, about 14 times a day, so a day's picture is a patchwork. Dark wedges between strips are areas the satellite did not pass over that day.

    Picture is the base: a normal photograph (true colour), the Earth at night, or the ocean coloured by its temperature. On top adds layers over it, each with its colour key under the map.

    Play steps through the days. Clouds move, fires spread and sea ice grows and shrinks with the seasons.

    Arctic and Antarctic. Near each pole the sun does not rise for weeks to months around midwinter (December in the Arctic, June in the Antarctic). Cameras need daylight, so the true-colour picture and the sea ice layer show a black area over the pole then; the night-lights layer still works.

    Latest is the most recent full day. Some layers take a day or two longer to arrive; a blank map on the newest days means that layer is not ready yet.

      How to read this

      The Earth is NASA's Blue Marble, a cloud-free picture made from many days of satellite images. It is turned to match this moment: the stars behind it are the stars over each place right now.

      Pins on the Earth are natural events NASA has reported in the last 30 days (wildfires, volcanoes, storms, ice), updated by NASA as they happen. See it on the Earth map opens the satellite picture for that day.

      Markers in the sky are galaxies (rings), nebulae (diamonds) and star clusters (dots) at their real positions among the stars: every Messier object, every galaxy bright enough for a backyard telescope (magnitude 12 or brighter) and every deep-sky object with a common name, about 1,350 in all. Famous ones are named; hover over or tap any marker for its name. Each opens NASA's pictures of it.

      Astronaut photos (blue squares) are photos of Earth taken from the space station, placed at the centre of what each one shows, from NASA's Gateway to Astronaut Photography of Earth; the newest are added every day.

      Constellations are drawn as faint lines between their main stars, to help find your way around.

      Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom. Far objects are drawn on the sky's sphere; their true distances run from hundreds to millions of light-years.

      Out under the real sky? Sky Finder turns a phone into a pointer for tonight's passing asteroids, the Moon, planets and space stations (how to use it).

        Results

        Type a mission, a planet, a galaxy or a place, or pick a mission. Results appear as you type.

        Launches, people, testing, events, and artists' illustrations: pictures about a mission rather than taken by it.

        How to read this

        Space pictures first. NASA's library holds both pictures taken by its missions (galaxies, planets, the Earth from orbit) and pictures about them (launches, engineers, events). The page reads each picture's record (its NASA ID, the centre that published it and the words in its caption) and shows the pictures taken in or of space first; the rest are under Behind the scenes. The sorting is automatic and sometimes wrong.

        NASA and ESA. Each search also asks ESA's Webb and Hubble picture archives (esawebb.org, esahubble.org), which hold every science picture the two telescopes' European teams have released. A tag on each picture says whose it is. From picks NASA's library, ESA's archives or both. Pictures and videos work for both (ESA's videos play from ESA's servers); the NASA centre filter applies to NASA's pictures; the years apply to ESA's pictures by the year in their ID (for example weic2622c is from 2026). Opening an ESA picture or video shows its credit line, read from ESA's page for it.

        Spelling. Suggestions appear as you type and allow for small mistakes ("hubbel" finds Hubble). When a search finds almost nothing, the closest known name is searched instead, and the page says so.

        Dates are the dates in NASA's records. For older pictures this can be the day the picture was added to the library, not the day it was taken; the caption usually gives the real date.

        NASA centre is the NASA site that published the picture, for example Johnson Space Center (JSC) for astronaut photos and Goddard (GSFC) for Earth-observing satellites.

        Up to 10,000 results can be shown for one search. A narrower search (a mission, a centre or a span of years) reaches the rest.

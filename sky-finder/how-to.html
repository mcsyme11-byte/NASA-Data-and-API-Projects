How to use it

Hold your phone up to the sky and Sky Finder marks where tonight's asteroid is, with its path, and the Moon, planets and bright stars around it to guide you. It is also reached from the Asteroid Tracker's "Visible from here" tab.

Step 1

Put it on your home screen

  1. SafariOpen Sky Finder in Safari.
  2. ShareTap the Share button: the square with an arrow.
  3. Add to Home ScreenChoose it in the list, then tap Add.
  1. ChromeOpen Sky Finder in Chrome.
  2. MenuTap ⋮ at the top right.
  3. Add to Home screenOr "Install app", depending on the phone.

It then opens full screen, like an app, and works with a weak connection once it has been opened online.

Step 2

Allow three things

  1. CameraTo show the sky behind the markers. Without it, the markers are drawn over a dark background.
  2. LocationTo work out what is above you. Or type your town instead.
  3. Motion and orientationTo know where the phone points. An iPhone asks when you first tap "Point at the sky". Without it, drag the sky with a finger.

All three are used on your phone only. Nothing is recorded, saved or sent: see the Privacy page.

Step 3

Find it

  1. Pick itTap the asteroid in tonight's list, or tap "Point at the sky" and choose it from the row of names in the camera view.
  2. Calibrate oncePut the Moon or a bright planet in the circle and tap Done. Phone compasses are often 5 to 15 degrees off, and magnetic north is not true north; this fixes both. It is kept for 12 hours, and the dot on the Calibrate button turns green.
  3. Follow the arrowTurn until the diamond is in the middle. The dotted line is where it will be, hour by hour.
  4. LookLook there with your eyes, binoculars or a telescope, as its tag says.
On the screen

What the marks mean

An asteroid: where it is now (the chosen one is larger, with a ring) Its path over the next hours, with the times A planet, or the Moon (a larger disc), to check you are pointed right A space station (the ISS or Tiangong): a bright star moving steadily when it is in sunlight Off the screen: turn this way The Sun: never look at it, least of all with binoculars or a telescope
The rules

What you need to see it

Naked eye
Magnitude up to 6, from a dark place.
Binoculars
Magnitude 6 to 9.
Small telescope
Magnitude 9 to 12 (8 to 15 cm across).
Large telescope
Magnitude 12 to 15 (20 cm and up).
Too faint
Above 15, for backyard viewing.

A bigger magnitude means fainter. Example: an asteroid at magnitude 10.4 needs a small telescope; under a town sky or a bright Moon, count it one step down the list (a large telescope).

Safety and tips

Good to know

  • Never look at the Sun, least of all with binoculars or a telescope. Sky Finder marks it in red.
  • Look where you walk, stay away from roads and edges, and never use it while driving.
  • Most asteroids are far too faint for a phone camera, so you see the marker rather than the asteroid. A few bright passes, like Apophis in April 2029, can be seen with the naked eye.
  • Go somewhere dark, give your eyes 20 minutes, and use Red light (top right) to keep them used to the dark.
  • Keep the phone away from metal and magnets, which upset the compass; calibrate again if the markers drift.

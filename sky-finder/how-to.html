Sky Finder

How to use it

Hold your phone up to the sky and Sky Finder marks where tonight's asteroid is, with its path and the planets around it to guide you.

1 · Put it on your home screen

  1. Open Sky Finder in Safari.
  2. Tap the Share button (the square with an arrow).
  3. Choose Add to Home Screen, then Add.
  1. Open Sky Finder in Chrome.
  2. Tap ⋮ (top right).
  3. Choose Add to Home screen or Install app.

It then opens full screen, like an app, and works with a weak connection once it has been opened online.

2 · Allow three things

  1. Camera, to show the sky behind the markers.
  2. Location, to work out what is above you. Or type your town instead.
  3. Motion and orientation, to know where the phone points. An iPhone asks for this when you first tap "Point at the sky".

All three stay on your phone. Nothing is sent or recorded; see the Privacy page. Without the camera the markers are drawn over a dark background; without the motion sensors you can drag the sky with a finger.

3 · Find it

  1. Pick the asteroid from tonight's list.
  2. Calibrate once: put the Moon or a bright planet in the circle and tap Done. Phone compasses are often 5 to 15 degrees off, and magnetic north is not true north; this fixes both. It is kept for 12 hours.
  3. Follow the arrow until the diamond is in the middle. The dotted line is where it will be, hour by hour.
  4. Look there with your eyes, binoculars or telescope, as the label says.

What the marks mean

An asteroid: where it is now (the chosen one is larger, with a ring) Its path over the next hours, with the times A planet, or the Moon (a larger disc), to check you are pointed right A space station (the ISS or Tiangong): a bright star moving steadily when it is in sunlight Off the screen: turn this way The Sun: never look at it, least of all with binoculars or a telescope

What you need to see it

  1. Naked eye magnitude up to 6, from a dark place
  2. Binoculars 6 to 9
  3. Small telescope 9 to 12 (8–15 cm)
  4. Large telescope 12 to 15 (20 cm and up)
  5. Too faint above 15, for backyard viewing

A bigger magnitude means fainter. A town sky or a bright Moon moves everything one step down the list.

Good to know

  1. Most asteroids are far too faint for a phone camera, so you will see the marker rather than the asteroid. A few bright passes, like Apophis in April 2029, can be seen with the naked eye.
  2. Go somewhere dark, give your eyes 20 minutes, and use Red light (top of the list) to keep them used to the dark.
  3. Keep the phone away from metal and magnets, which upset the compass; calibrate again if the markers drift.
  4. Look where you walk, stay away from roads, and never use it while driving.
